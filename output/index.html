    Buttigieg warns Trump's fuel-economy rollback could cost drivers $1,600 more

    Former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg told NPR that weaker fuel-economy standards could leave U.S. automakers behind China and raise long-term costs for drivers, despite the administration's claims of affordability.

    The Trump administration's rollback of fuel-economy standards could backfire on drivers, according to Pete Buttigieg, the former U.S. Transportation Secretary who served under Biden. While the administration claims the new rules will cut vehicle prices by about $1,300, Buttigieg pointed out that drivers could pay roughly $1,600 more in fuel costs over a vehicle's lifetime—a calculation that doesn't account for higher gas prices or tariffs affecting the auto industry.

    Buttigieg's concerns extend beyond consumer costs. He warned that weaker standards could slow U.S. auto innovation as China expands its electric vehicle industry and competes for a larger share of the global market. "Anything that means less innovation is going to help hand that advantage to China," he said in an NPR interview. The rollback undermines the competitiveness of American automakers at a time when the global automotive industry is shifting toward electrification.

    American automakers have broadly welcomed the rollback, arguing that previous standards were out of step with consumer demand. However, Buttigieg noted that the industry has historically resisted tougher fuel-economy rules before ultimately adapting to them. The Corporate Average Fuel Economy standards require carmakers to meet fuel-economy benchmarks as an average across their entire fleets. Under the new system taking effect in 2030, the Trump administration is replacing outdated 1975-era classification criteria with a simpler measurement based on towing and payload capacity.
