📰 Full Coverage & Verified Details:

The Pacific Ocean is experiencing an extraordinarily active hurricane season while the Atlantic remains in an eerie calm, a stark reversal driven by El Niño's powerful grip on global weather systems.

According to NPR, the Pacific has recorded an unusual number of storms this year, with multiple named hurricanes including Polo, Odalys, and Nolo tracked by forecasters. Meanwhile, the Atlantic has not recorded a single full-blown hurricane this season—a dramatic departure from typical seasonal expectations. Scientists attribute this dramatic divergence to an exceptionally strong El Niño phenomenon.

El Niño, characterized by warmer-than-normal ocean temperatures in the central and eastern Pacific, fundamentally alters atmospheric conditions across ocean basins. The Weather Channel's hurricane season maps tracker shows active monitoring of multiple tropical systems in the Pacific, including the aforementioned named storms and additional areas of concern. This seasonal disparity underscores how significantly oceanic temperature patterns influence hurricane development and distribution worldwide.

The contrast between Pacific activity and Atlantic dormancy highlights the complex interplay between ocean-atmosphere interactions and seasonal storm formation. Warmer Pacific waters fuel convection and storm development, while El Niño typically suppresses Atlantic hurricane activity by increasing wind shear—the change in wind speed and direction at different altitudes that tears apart forming storms.

Forecasters continue monitoring tropical systems as the 2026 season evolves, with The Weather Channel and other agencies providing real-time tracking and forecasts for affected regions. This season serves as a stark reminder of how global climate patterns can dramatically reshape regional weather outcomes.
