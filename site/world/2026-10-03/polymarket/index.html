Polymarket Traders Bet $77,507 on Potential Collapse of Major Global Banks

By Karim Fouad · Published 03 Oct 2026, 10:30 UTC

Financial regulators are monitoring Polymarket as users place high-stakes bets on the failure of institutions including HSBC and Lloyds before 2027.

Quick facts

Total wagered amount
$77,507
Target institutions
HSBC, Lloyds, JP Morgan, BNP Paribas
Deadline for bets
End of 2026
Participating reach
Approximately 150 countries

Traders have wagered $77,507 on whether massive financial institutions, including JP Morgan, BNP Paribas, HSBC, and Lloyds Banking Group, will collapse before the end of 2026. This activity takes place on the US-owned offshore prediction market platform Polymarket, which allows users from approximately 150 countries to profit financially from events that could threaten global economic stability.

Financial regulators and lawmakers express significant concern that these speculative contracts could invite market manipulation or fuel social media-driven bank runs. The collapses of Silicon Valley Bank and Credit Suisse in 2023 demonstrate how quickly public confidence can erode when frenzies of speculation circulate online. Bobby Dean, a Liberal Democrat Member of Parliament and Treasury committee member, warned that such platforms lack robust safeguards against insider trading and could be exploited to manipulate market sentiment.

While residents in the UK, US, Canada, and the EU are currently restricted from participating on the platform, authorities fear the risks posed by these bets are systemic. The UK’s Financial Conduct Authority confirmed it is actively consulting with international regulators to address the integrity of prediction markets. Dean urged officials to prioritize cross-border cooperation to mitigate these financial risks before they escalate, emphasizing that the sector moves with dangerous speed.

As the industry monitors the situation, the focus remains on preventing bad actors from using the platform to trigger panic. Regulators maintain that even small-scale activities today could grow into broader threats to public confidence in major lenders.
