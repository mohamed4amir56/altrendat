Super El Niño smashes sea surface temperature records before expected December peak

By Karim Fouad · Published 13 Oct 2026, 13:30 UTC

Record-breaking sea-surface temperatures in the central Pacific have reached unprecedented levels as the current El Niño event continues to strengthen.

Quick facts

Expected peak
December
Observed temperature
Above 30C
Comparison benchmark
2015 El Niño

Temperatures exceeding 30C have been recorded in parts of the tropical Pacific for the first time, signaling a shift that experts describe as unprecedented. While El Niño is a natural climate pattern that typically occurs every two to seven years, this year's event has surpassed the daily records set during the intense 2015 period, according to data from Australia's Bureau of Meteorology. Scientists note that the ocean warmth is not confined to the traditional tropical zones but is spreading into the Gulf of Mexico and across wider Pacific regions.

The implications of these record temperatures are significant, as El Niño often drives major shifts in global weather patterns. The Met Office reports that many expected impacts are already being felt worldwide. Because human-induced climate change is warming the oceans alongside these natural cycles, researchers are utilizing the Relative Ocean El Niño Index to gain a more accurate comparison of the event's true strength against historical milestones like the 1997-98 and 2015-16 cycles.

Data from the Bureau of Meteorology indicates that average weekly sea-surface temperatures in the central Pacific have hit record territory well ahead of the event's anticipated peak in December. Dr. Zeke Hausfather of Berkeley Earth states that the current event is distinct because its influence is spilling out of the traditional monitoring region, creating a broader thermal footprint than previous major climate events. Long-term ocean warming is contributing to these figures, yet scientists emphasize that this represents only a portion of the observed intensity.

While the current event continues to strengthen, official forecasters are maintaining close watch on the equatorial Pacific to monitor further developments. The international scientific community continues to analyze these measurements to understand how this year's pattern compares to the established historical record as the December peak approaches.
