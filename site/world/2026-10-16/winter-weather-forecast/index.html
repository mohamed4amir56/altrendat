Super El Niño triggers storm surge across Southern United States this winter

By Karim Fouad · Published 16 Oct 2026, 19:31 UTC

NOAA forecasts an active, storm-heavy winter pattern across the Southern U.S. driven by an 83% chance of a record-breaking Super El Niño.

Quick facts

Meteorological Winter Start
December 1
Super El Niño Probability
83%
Temperature Threshold
2 degrees Celsius above normal

Central equatorial Pacific water temperatures surpassed 2 degrees Celsius above normal during September, pushing current conditions into the high-stakes territory known as a Super El Niño. NOAA reports there is now an 83% likelihood that this season will become the strongest event of its kind since official record-keeping began in 1950, a notable jump from the 75% probability estimated just one month prior. This atmospheric shift essentially acts as a conveyor belt, extending the Pacific jet stream and directing a higher frequency of storms across the Southern Tier of the country. Residents from Southern California through the Southern Plains and into Florida should prepare for a particularly wet season as this climate phase takes hold. While the increased precipitation is expected to provide much-needed relief for drought-afflicted regions, NOAA officials have issued warnings that the influx of moisture onto dry, parched soil creates an elevated risk for destructive flash floods and sudden mudslides. Beyond the storm-prone South, the broader seasonal forecast points toward warmer-than-average temperatures settling over the majority of the remaining United States. Meteorological winter officially commences on December 1 and spans through the end of February, marking the period during which these El Niño-driven patterns are expected to exert their greatest influence. The event is defined by the El Niño-Southern Oscillation cycle, where significantly warmer water in the equatorial Pacific fundamentally alters global atmospheric circulation and shifts traditional storm tracks. Forecasters will continue to monitor whether sea surface temperatures remain at least 2 degrees above average for a three-month duration, the final threshold required to formally classify the season as a sustained Super El Niño event.
